---
title: Activities & transactions
description: Human-readable financial events backed by explicit ledger movements.
---

An **activity** is the event you recognize: salary, groceries, rent, a transfer,
or an investment purchase. A **transaction** is the accounting statement that
moves an amount from one account to another.

Keeping these concepts separate gives one event a readable identity while
allowing it to contain as many ledger movements as it needs.

## Transactions are the source of truth

Each transaction contains:

- an amount;
- a source account;
- a destination account;
- optional asset or counterparty references on either side;
- optional fund legs.

For example:

| Activity        | From         | To              | Meaning                       |
| --------------- | ------------ | --------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Salary          | Revenue      | Bank account    | Earned value entered the bank |
| Groceries       | Bank account | Expense         | Value was consumed            |
| Move to savings | Bank account | Savings account | Value changed location        |

The transfer is neither revenue nor expense because neither transaction side is
a revenue or expense account.

## Types are derived, not selected

Maille derives an activity's types from the account types touched by its
transactions:

- an Expense leg contributes `expense`;
- a Revenue leg contributes `revenue`;
- an Investment account leg contributes `investment`;
- an Assets leg contributes `asset`;
- a transaction without a typed side contributes `neutral`.

An activity can have several types. This is important because classification
cannot contradict the ledger: changing the transactions changes the derived
type and amount together.

## Activity status

An activity is:

- `scheduled` when its date is in the future;
- `incomplete` when transaction totals on movement-tracked accounts are not
  fully explained by linked movements;
- `completed` when those reconciliation totals agree.

Accounts without movement tracking do not require bank evidence. This lets
cash-only or conceptual entries complete without manufacturing a movement.

## Why activities can contain several transactions

Real events are often compound. A purchase can involve a bank account, an
expense account, an asset, a shared counterparty, and multiple fund legs. One
activity keeps that context together while its transactions preserve the exact
accounting effect.

Categories, subcategories, and projects belong to the activity because they
describe the event. Account and fund movements belong to transactions because
they describe the financial effect.
