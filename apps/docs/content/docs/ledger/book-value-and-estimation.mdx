---
title: Book value & estimation
description: The ledger records cost; observations record worth. Only a sale turns one into the other.
---

A personal ledger must hold two numbers about anything whose value changes by
itself:

- **Book value** — what the ledger says: the money that entered minus the
  money that left, through dated transactions.
- **Estimated value** — what an observation says: a market price, an
  appraisal, a trusted comparison, dated on a timeline.

Maille keeps the two in separate layers and never converts an observation
into a ledger entry. This page explains why that boundary is the single most
consequential design decision in the valuation model.

## Book value is transactional truth

An investment account's balance is the net of the cash you moved in and out
of it. An asset's book value is the money it received minus the money it gave.
Both are auditable: every cent of them traces to a transaction leg. Book value
answers "what did I put into this?" — which is the only question a ledger can
answer with certainty.

## Estimations are observations on a timeline

An estimated value is a **dated observation**, not a field that gets
overwritten:

- an investment price is an observation of a unit price, one per day, with
  the latest replacing the same-day one;
- an asset valuation is an observation of the whole object's worth.

The estimation in effect at any date is the latest observation at or before
it. That rule makes history possible: a position can be valued as of last
quarter using the prices that existed then, and an appraisal from two years
ago does not silently vanish when a newer one arrives. An unpriced position
has **no** estimation — absence is displayed as absence, never as zero,
because "we do not know" and "it is worth nothing" are different facts.

Estimation for an account sums the priced positions. It carries the date of
the observation it rests on and its unrealized difference against book value,
so you always know both how fresh the market's opinion is and how far it has
drifted from cost.

## Why observations never book

Booking a market gain would create revenue with no transaction behind it. It
would violate double entry — value appearing in a bank account without moving
from anywhere — and it would make income mean something other than "value
that actually reached me". The consequences compound:

- **Revenue would become an opinion.** Realized income and mark-to-market
  gains would be indistinguishable in every report.
- **The ledger would forecast.** A schedule that grows a value by assumption
  keeps claiming income in the months the market disagrees with it. This is
  why Maille has depreciation and deliberately no reverse depreciation: use
  can be spread arithmetically, but gain is a fact only a buyer can create.
- **Balances would drift from the bank.** Book value is what reconciles
  against statements. Rebook it with market noise and reconciliation loses
  its meaning.

So the unrealized difference is displayed — next to the balance, with its
observation date — and nothing more.

## Realization: where the layers meet

The only moment an observation's gain becomes ledger fact is a sale. Selling
a position moves value from the investment account back to a bank account at
the price you actually obtained: the difference between your book value and
the sale proceeds is a realized gain or loss, recorded through the same
double-entry machinery as any other event. Realization is when the market's
opinion is replaced by a counterparty's payment.

The same logic covers assets: an appraisal shown as an estimation never books,
and the gain relative to book value is realized only when the asset is sold.
