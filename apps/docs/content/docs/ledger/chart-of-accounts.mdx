---
title: Chart of accounts
description: How accounts are classified into balances and flows, and how Maille's types map to classic bookkeeping.
---

A chart of accounts is the vocabulary of a ledger: the fixed set of locations
value can be held, owed, earned, or spent. Every transaction names two of
them, so the quality of your reports is decided when you choose the accounts,
not when you read them.

Maille's chart of accounts has seven types, in two families:

| Family  | Types                                       | Question answered                | Money sits in them?      |
| ------- | ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Balance | bank, investment, cash, assets, liabilities | Where value is, and what is owed | Yes, as a position       |
| Flow    | revenue, expense                            | Why value changed                | No, value passed through |

Balance accounts are **stocks**: they hold a position at every point in time.
Flow accounts are **flows**: they accumulate what passed through them over a
period. The two families behave differently in every report, which is why the
distinction is worth internalizing before you create accounts.

## Where net worth lives

Classic bookkeeping has an equity section: the owner's stake in the business.
Maille deliberately has no equity account. Net worth is computed — the sum of
balance accounts minus what is owed — because an account you edit by hand can
disagree with the balances it summarizes, and a derived number cannot. For the
same reason, revenues and expenses are not "closed into equity" at year end in
Maille: they remain permanent, queryable records of flows, and any period's
net-worth change can be recomputed from them.

## Choosing granularity

The practical question for a personal chart of accounts is how many accounts
to create. The ledger's answer is: create accounts where the **financial
treatment differs**, use categories where only the **reporting label**
differs.

- Two bank accounts with separate statements are two accounts: each needs its
  own balance and its own reconciliation against its own movements.
- "Groceries" and "Household supplies" are one Expense account with two
  categories: they differ in how you want to report them, not in how money
  moves.
- A loan with a real repayment schedule is a liabilities account: its balance
  is a fact you will want next to your assets.
- A flatmate's share of expenses is not an account at all: it is a
  counterparty on transaction legs, from which shared liability is computed.

Over-creating accounts makes reconciliation heavier (more movements to match)
without improving any report. Under-creating accounts collapses facts that
differ — putting a brokerage account and your wallet in one account makes
"money I can spend today" unanswerable.

## The role of each type

Each account type has a dedicated page under
[Accounts](/docs/financial/accounts), with the actions it supports and their
implications:

- [Bank accounts](/docs/financial/accounts/bank-accounts) — money an
  institution holds and reports on, the reconciliation surface of the ledger.
- [Investment accounts](/docs/financial/accounts/investment-accounts) — value
  held for return, with positions and a price timeline.
- [Cash](/docs/financial/accounts/cash) — money only you can count.
- [Assets](/docs/financial/accounts/assets) — objects owned and used, carried
  at cost and depreciated.
- [Liabilities](/docs/financial/accounts/liabilities) — what you owe, as a
  first-class balance.
- [Expenses](/docs/financial/accounts/expenses) and
  [Revenues](/docs/financial/accounts/revenues) — the flow accounts that
  explain changes.

## Movement tracking is per account

An account either expects external evidence or it does not. Bank and broker
accounts you import statements for have movement tracking enabled; conceptual
accounts like Expense or a wallet have it disabled. The setting decides when
activities touching the account are considered complete — see
[Movements & reconciliation](/docs/financial/movements-and-reconciliation).
Choosing it is part of designing your chart of accounts, because it determines
which balances you promise to prove against the outside world.
