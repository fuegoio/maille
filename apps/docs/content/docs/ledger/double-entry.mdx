---
title: Double entry
description: Every movement of value has two sides, and what that rule buys a personal ledger.
---

Double entry is the rule that value never appears or disappears: it always
moves **from** somewhere **to** somewhere. A transaction in Maille is one such
movement — an amount, a source account, and a destination account. The
balances of both accounts change by exactly that amount, in opposite
directions, and nothing else in the ledger changes.

Classic bookkeeping expresses this with debits and credits. Maille expresses
it with directions: money leaves the `from` account and enters the `to`
account. The discipline is identical — the two sides must name the accounts
and the amount must be the same on both — only the vocabulary differs, because
for personal finance "from my bank to my landlord's expense" reads more
naturally than "debit rent expense, credit bank".

## The personal accounting equation

Bookkeeping's fundamental equation is:

```text
Assets = Liabilities + Equity
```

For a person, equity is **net worth**, and Maille derives it rather than
storing it:

```text
Net worth = (bank + cash + investments + assets) − liabilities
```

Revenue and expense accounts sit in a second equation that explains how net
worth changed between two dates:

```text
Net worth change = Revenues − Expenses
```

A salary transaction moves value from a revenue account into a bank account.
The revenue account is not a place money sits — it is the source the value
arrived through. Six months later, summing the revenue accounts tells you
exactly why your net worth grew by that much, and summing balances tells you
where it ended up. The two views must agree because they are derived from the
same transactions.

## The four things value can do

Every transaction in a personal ledger is one of four movements:

| Movement        | From                                                                      | To              | Effect on net worth |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------- | ------------------- |
| Earn            | Revenue                                                                   | Balance account | Increases           |
| Spend           | Balance account                                                           | Expense         | Decreases           |
| Move (transfer) | Balance account                                                           | Balance account | None                |
| Convert or owe  | Balance or liability account with an asset, investment, or liability side | Depends         | None until realized |

The third row is the one personal tools most often get wrong. Moving money
from a current account to a savings account, or withdrawing cash, is not
spending: neither side is an expense account, so the transaction is neutral
and no report counts it as consumption. Classification follows the ledger,
never the other way around — an activity is an expense because one of its
transactions names an expense account, not because a label says so.

The fourth row is where double entry earns its keep. Buying shares moves money
from a bank account to an investment account: your net worth does not change,
the composition of it does. Taking a loan moves value from a liability account
(the debt you now owe) into your bank account: you are not richer, and the
ledger says so because the liability balance grew by the same amount the bank
balance did.

## Compound events: one activity, several transactions

Real events rarely fit one movement. Paying a shared grocery bill by card,
partly for yourself and partly for a flatmate, involves: the bank account (the
money), an expense account (the consumption), and a counterparty leg (the part
the flatmate now owes you). Maille keeps the event as one **activity** — the
human-readable identity, with its date, name, and category — and lets it carry
several **transactions**, each an exact double-entry movement. The activity is
what you recognize; the transactions are what is true in the accounting sense.

## What the rule buys

- **Balances cannot drift.** A balance is not a number someone set once and
  edited since; it is the sum of opening state plus every transaction. A
  displayed balance can always be audited down to the entries.
- **Errors are visible.** Money that entered without a source or left without
  a destination has no valid representation — the mistake surfaces as an
  unexplained reconciliation difference rather than as a wrong total.
- **Reports are consistent by construction.** Spending, income, and net worth
  all derive from the same transactions, so they cannot disagree with each
  other.
- **Direction carries meaning.** Because legs say from and to, a shared
  expense can distinguish the part you consumed from the part someone owes
  you, and a liability can be tracked to the euro without trusting a name in
  an account label.
